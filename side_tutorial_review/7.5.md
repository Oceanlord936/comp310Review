//7.5 — How these connect to T01 vs. what's still ahead

in T01 (the address-space orientation lab, which built the Part 4 material) you already ran walker, alloc, and grind as part of exploring the address space and confirming the environment worked. At that point they were mostly "run this, observe the address-space layout it produces" — you weren't yet asked to reason deeply about the kernel mechanisms behind them.

---

What Part 7 just did differently: it went back and filled in why each program behaves the way it does — walker needs sbrkexec because it needs executable code spread across pages; alloc needs sbrklazy because it's demonstrating the fault-driven allocation path from 2.6; grind/forktest are stress nets rather than teaching demos. So Part 7 isn't new lab work, it's retroactively explaining the tools you already touched.

================

What's still ahead: the (future)-tagged memory-management labs (mentioned at the bottom of Part 6 and implicitly here) will come back to alloc and lazy allocation in much more depth — actual page-fault-handler code, physical page allocation bookkeeping, maybe copy-on-write. For now, you have the conceptual hooks (sbrk vs sbrklazy vs sbrkexec, and the lazy-allocation exception path) — Part 8 (sysinfo syscall) doesn't need you to go any deeper on memory than this.
