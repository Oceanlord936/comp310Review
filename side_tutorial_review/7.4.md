//7.4 — grind and forktest: stress/regression tests you run, not read closely

# These two are qualitatively different from spin/busy/walker/alloc — those four each demonstrate one specific mechanism

grind and forktest aren't demonstrations
— they're regression/stress tests:

programs that exercise a wide combination of kernel operations rapidly and repeatedly, specifically to catch breakage.

===============
forktest: hammers fork() specifically

creating many child processes in rapid succession (and often nested), checking that the kernel's process-table management, PID allocation, and memory-space duplication

# all hold up under volume. If you've just modified anything touching process creation (e.g. while doing the T02 syscall lab, or any future process-table work), forktest is what tells you whether you broke it.

grind:

a broader stress test that combines multiple system operations together — forking, exiting, waiting, exec, file operations, etc.

— thrown together somewhat randomly/repeatedly to surface race conditions, resource leaks

(e.g. a zombie leak from 5.6 that eventually exhausts the process table), or edge cases that a single, clean test wouldn't trigger. It's a "if the kernel can survive this chaos, it's probably fine" sanity check.

---

The practical point for you:
after you make changes to the kernel (like adding the sysinfo syscall in Part 8), you don't read grind's or forktest's source closely or reason about what each line does — you just run them and check they still pass/complete without crashing or hanging.

They're regression nets, not teaching material. This is different from walker/alloc, which you actually care about understanding.
