6.6 — Priority scheduling, starvation, and aging

=============
Priority scheduling:

every process gets a priority number, and the scheduler always runs the highest-priority ready process.
(Convention varies — some systems say lower number = higher priority, e.g. 0 is most urgent; xv6-style and most textbooks tend to use that convention, but always check which way a given problem defines it.)

=================

Notice SJF and SRTN are actually special cases of priority scheduling:

s priority = predicted burst length (shorter burst = higher priority).
So everything from 6.4/6.5 — needing a prediction, preemptive vs. non-preemptive variants — carries over here too. Priority scheduling can be either:

1. Non-preemptive: once a process starts running, it finishes its burst even if a higher-priority process arrives (checked only at moments 1/4).

2. Preemptive: a newly arrived higher-priority process immediately preempts the current one (checked at moment 3 too, same as SRTN's logic).

=======================

Starvation: the fatal flaw. If priorities are static and there's a steady stream of high-priority processes arriving, a low-priority process can sit in the ready queue indefinitely
— never getting picked

---

Aging: the standard fix. Instead of keeping priority fixed forever, gradually increase a process's priority the longer it waits in the ready queue. Eventually, even a process that started at the lowest priority will age up enough to become the highest-priority process in the system and get its turn.

This guarantees an upper bound on wait time — no process is starved indefinitely — while still normally favoring genuinely high-priority work in the short term.
