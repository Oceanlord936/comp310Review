//6.4 — SJF (Shortest Job First),
provably optimal, and why we predict with exponential averaging

SJF's rule: of the processes currently in the ready queue, run whichever has the smallest next CPU burst first.

=============================

Why it's provably optimal: for a fixed set of jobs, SJF minimizes average waiting time. The intuition — if you run a short job before a long one, the short job's wait barely increases the long job's wait, but if you run the long job first, every short job behind it eats the full delay (this is the convoy effect from 6.3, generalized).

===========================

The catch
— it needs the future.
SJF requires knowing each process's next CPU burst length before running it. But the OS can't know that

— a process could run for 2ms or 200ms and nothing about its past behavior directly reveals which. This makes pure SJF non-implementable as stated;
it's mostly a theoretical lower bound to measure other algorithms against.

=======================

The fix: predict, don't know. The OS estimates the next burst length using the process's history of past bursts, via exponential averaging:

τ(n+1) = α · t(n) + (1 − α) · τ(n)

---

t(n) = the actual length of the burst that just finished

τ(n) = the previous predicted length for that burst

τ(n+1) = the new prediction for the next burst

α (0 ≤ α ≤ 1) = how much weight recent history gets vs. older history

=======================

α near 1 → the prediction reacts almost entirely to the most recent burst (fast-adapting, noisy).

α near 0 → the prediction barely moves, dominated by long-run history (stable, slow to adapt).

---

It's called "exponential" because if you expand the recurrence, older terms get weighted by (1−α) raised to increasing powers — their influence decays exponentially the further back they are.
