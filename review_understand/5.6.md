//5.6 — Zombies and orphans
This section is really two separate "loose end" cases that both come from the same fact

a process's lifecycle depends on its parent being around and paying attention. What happens when one side of that relationship breaks?

=========================

Zombie = child that exited, but parent hasn't called wait() yet.

Already covered the mechanics in 5.5 — after exit(), the process is dead (memory/files freed) but its table slot lingers, holding just:

its PID
its exit status
its state marked ZOMBIE

=========================

Orphan = parent that exited (or was killed) while its child was still running.

This is the mirror-image problem: now there's a live child whose parent no longer exists to ever call wait() on it. If nothing were done, that child would become a zombie forever the moment it exits — no one left who could reap it.

=======================
the fix,
in both xv6 and Linux: when a process exits, the kernel reparents all of its children to init

(PID 1, the very first process started at boot).
init's job includes looping forever, calling wait() on whatever shows up as its child — so orphaned children (and orphaned zombies) eventually get reaped by init instead of their original parent.

This is why init can never itself exit while the system is running: it's the reclaimer of last resort.

=====================
Putting 5.5 and 5.6 together — why a zombie leak eventually stops the machine from starting anything:

Take xv6's fixed 64-slot process table. Suppose some buggy program repeatedly fork()s children that exit, but the parent never calls wait()
(or the parent itself exits without being init, and somehow reparenting doesn't happen fast enough / init is also swamped).

Each of those exited children stays a zombie, permanently occupying one of the 64 slots — because "occupying a slot" is exactly what being a zombie means in 5.5's model.

Do this enough times, and all 64 slots fill with zombies. At that point, fork() — which needs a free slot to write the new PCB into — fails for every process on the system,

ncluding ones completely unrelated to the buggy program. Since even the shell forking a new command needs a free slot, you can reach a state where you can't even start ls.

That's the "stops the machine from starting anything" line from the plan: not a crash, just total exhaustion of a small, fixed resource, one unreaped corpse at a time.
