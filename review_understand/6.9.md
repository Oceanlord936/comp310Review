//6.9 — Lottery scheduling:
the algorithm, ticket mechanisms, and why it's fair on average but not short-run

The algorithm:

every process holds some number of tickets (from 6.8's proportional-share idea).
At each scheduling decision, the scheduler picks a "random number "
in the range of all outstanding tickets (a "lottery draw"),
finds which process holds the ticket matching that number, and runs it for one quantum (this is Round-Robin-style, quantum-based — just the selection is randomized instead of FIFO).

---

Example:
A has 50 tickets, B has 30, C has 20 (total 100). Draw a random number 0–99. If it lands in [0,50) → A runs; [50,80) → B runs; [80,100) → C runs.
'

Over many draws, the law of large numbers means A wins roughly 50% of draws, B roughly 30%, C roughly 20% — matching their proportional shares from 6.8, without ever needing to track history or compute exact ratios directly. Probability does the enforcement for you.

================

Ticket mechanisms — ways processes can manipulate their own share, useful for cooperating processes:

1. Ticket currency:
   a process (or user) can allocate its own tickets to sub-processes it owns, in its own local currency, which the scheduler converts to global tickets proportionally.
   E.g. a user with 100 global tickets can give their two child processes "500 local tickets" each, and the scheduler just divides them back down to the user's real global share — the local numbering is just a convenience layer, it doesn't let you cheat the system.

2. Ticket transfer:
   a process can temporarily hand its tickets to another process it's waiting on

— e.g.,
if process A is blocked waiting for process B to do work on A's behalf
(like a client waiting on a server), A can transfer its tickets to B so B gets scheduled sooner, since running B is effectively how A makes progress. Without this, a process holding lots of tickets but sitting idle-and-waiting wastes its allocation.

3. Ticket inflation: a trusted process can temporarily create more tickets for itself, boosting its own relative share. This only makes sense among mutually trusting processes (it's abusable otherwise) — it's a lightweight way to say "I need more CPU right now" without renegotiating with a central authority.

=====================

Why fair on average, not short-run:

the proportional guarantee is a statistical one — it only converges as the number of draws grows large, the same way flipping a fair coin 10 times can easily give you 7 heads even though the "true" ratio is 50/50.

A process with 50% of tickets could, by bad luck, lose several draws in a row and get zero CPU time for a while, or conversely win an unlikely streak and hog the CPU early on. Over enough scheduling decisions this evens out to match the stated ratio, but there's no guarantee about any specific short window — unlike a deterministic algorithm (coming in 6.10) that can guarantee the ratio holds even over short intervals.
