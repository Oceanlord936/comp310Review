//6.3 — FCFS and the convoy effect

FCFS (First-Come, First-Served)

is the simplest possible policy: whoever arrives in the ready queue first runs first, straight through to completion, no preemption at all
(it's a moment-1/4-only scheduler from 6.2 — never takes the CPU away from a running process).

Implementation-wise it's just a FIFO queue: new arrivals go to the tail, the scheduler always picks the head.

=================
The convoy effect:

is FCFS's signature failure.
Imagine one CPU-bound process with a huge burst (say 100ms) arrives first, followed immediately by several short I/O-bound processes (say 1ms each). Because FCFS never preempts, every one of those short processes has to sit and wait behind the long one

— even though each would finish almost instantly if run first. The short processes' waiting time gets inflated, and since those short processes are usually about to do I/O, the I/O device sits idle that whole time too — so overall utilization (both CPU and I/O) tanks.

It's called a "convoy" because it's like a line of cars stuck behind one slow truck:
everyone behind is delayed regardless of how fast they themselves are.

Concretely, this hits average waiting time hard — FCFS's average waiting time is very sensitive to arrival order. Put the long job first and everyone's average wait balloons; put it last and average wait drops a lot, even though total work done is identical.
That order-sensitivity (versus SJF's guarantee, next section) is the main takeaway.
